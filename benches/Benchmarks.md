# Benchmarks

## Methodology

Publisher and subscriber run as separate processes on one host, both reading
`CLOCK_REALTIME`, so one-way latency is comparable without a clock-sync protocol.
That holds same-host only; cross-machine numbers need their own design.

Each subject is sent 8000 messages at 2000 Hz and 3000 samples are collected.
Every subject carries the same 16-byte header — sequence number and send timestamp —
so all of them are measured identically. TARWYN is published at 1000 Hz because it
does not keep up at 2000 Hz.

NetworkTables is configured to be measured at its best rather than at its defaults:
`sendAll(true)`, `keepDuplicates(true)`, `periodic(0.001s)`, `pollStorage(1000)`,
`flush()` after every `set`, read via `readQueue()`, and a subscriber that spins
rather than sleeps between polls. Its 100 ms default sweep is a configuration, not a
ceiling, and benchmarking against it would be misleading.

All subjects in a table are measured back to back in a single run, so they share
machine conditions and are comparable to each other. Absolute figures are sensitive
to load — the same subjects measured individually on an idle machine come out roughly
30% lower — so compare within a table rather than across runs.

Source: [benches/src](src), [benches/java/src](java/src). Generated by [generate.sh](generate.sh).

## Results

`P[NUMBER]` = [NUMBER] Percentile. All figures in microseconds, lower is better.
`Loss` is the share of published messages that never arrived, counted from gaps in
the sequence numbers.

## Reading the numbers

**tarwyn-rust's loss is the send high water mark, not congestion or slow joining.**
The subscriber receives sequence 0, so nothing is missed at startup. The client
publishes without waiting (ZMQ_DONTWAIT) and a send high water mark of 500, so once 500
messages are queued the rest are discarded rather than blocking the caller. Raising
the mark to 20000 takes loss to zero and the median from 77us to 94us: the queue
absorbs the burst but every message then waits behind it. That is the whole
reliability-versus-latency trade in one knob, and it is why the design splits
telemetry from control rather than picking one setting for both.

**nt4-flush loses nothing because it queues instead.** That queuing is what its
median measures. It is the only subject here that never discards.

**tarwyn-java's upper percentiles are JVM warmup.** The first messages are
interpreted before the JIT compiles the hot path, which is why its P95 and P100 sit
two orders of magnitude above its median. The median is the representative figure.

**udp-floor is the floor, not a product.** It carries no topics, no discovery and no
reliability. It exists to show how much of the gap above it is inherent to networking
and how much is the transport design.

**tarwyn-udp is the same broker with ZeroMQ removed from the telemetry path.**
Publishers send a fixed 16-byte header over UDP straight to the server, which fans
the datagram out to registered subscribers. Registration still goes over the existing
request/reply socket, so this is the control-versus-telemetry split rather than a
wholesale replacement. It is the measured payoff of the zmq-direct finding below.

**zmq-direct isolates the cost of ZeroMQ itself.** It is one hop, publisher straight to
subscriber with no broker, carrying the same protobuf envelope tarwyn-rust uses. The
gap from udp-floor up to zmq-direct is what ZeroMQ and protobuf cost per hop; the gap
from zmq-direct up to tarwyn-rust is what the broker relay costs. Both are large, and
the first is larger.
